# Mechanical Subsystem Reflection
--- 
This document presents our group's reflection on the mechanical subsystem and core lessons learnt from the design,protyping and iteration process. 

---

## Core Lessons : 

#### 1. Tolerancing: 
For the mechanical team, this was our first experience with both design and manufacturing, which led to several challenges related to tolerancing. As many of our components were manually fabricated using an acrylic bender, we encountered inconsistencies arising from measurement inaccuracies, material deformation, and warpage during the bending process. These factors made it difficult to achieve the exact dimensions specified in our CAD models. In hindsight, greater consideration should have been given to potential manufacturing variations during the design phase .We could have incorporated contingencies to accommodate such inaccuracies, such as allowing for adjustable features or planning for post-fabrication modifications.(e.g using hot air gun to adjust acrylic geometry). Moving forward, we have learned to better manage expectations and avoid assuming that fabricated parts will perfectly match their CAD specifications, instead allowing for tolerances and flexibility in both design and assembly

#### 2. Design Simplicity vs Risk Trade Off
Going in to this project, we anticipated that the integration process would be the most arduous one and so in view of that we approached the design of the mechanical subsystem to be as simple as possible with minimal electrical components. As such, we tried using a gravity fed system with the main launching mechanism being a 12V solenoid. However, by relying on a passive, gravity-fed mechanism, we unintentionally introduced variability that could not be easily regulated or corrected, leading to inconsistent system performance. For example, we had little control of how the balls fall into the launcher and whether it would fall within the maximum stroke length of the solenoid. As a result, we struggled initially to have consistent launches as there many minor factors that were not taken account for in the design and had to make adjustment spontaneously using bits and scraps lying around. Ultimately, this reinforced the importance of evaluating trade-offs between simplicity and robustness early in the design process, ensuring that key functionalities are sufficiently controlled to meet performance requirements.

#### 3. Packaging with constraints
Similar to the earlier point on design simplicity, our decision to rely heavily on off-the-shelf components introduced additional constraints. While this approach reduced fabrication effort, it limited our flexibility as we had to design around fixed dimensions and form factors. This significantly restricted our options during the packaging phase, as components could not be easily modified to better suit the overall system layout. As a result, much of our design process involved adapting our mechanical subsystem to fit within the existing TurtleBot3 structure. However, we quickly realised that even minor adjustments in component placement had cascading effects on other subsystems, such as electrical wiring routes and docking alignment. These interdependencies made it challenging to optimise the design in isolation. As such we came to learn that packaging is a very delicate skill and every design decision carries broader implications and must be evaluated not only for its local benefits but also for its impact on the overall system.
